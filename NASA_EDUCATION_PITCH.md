# How Outpost Down can help NASA

**What to say in the presentation:** Outpost Down gives kids a first experience of what aerospace teams do. They see a problem, read an instrument, work with their crew, and check that a repair made a difference. Brushing dust off a solar array gives “power budget” a meaning: restored electricity can keep the habitat running. Fixing the return ship gives terms such as “power bus” and “CO₂ scrubber” a purpose. Each repair points to a real mission and a job that uses the same kind of thinking. That early familiarity can help kids picture themselves in aerospace and choose to explore it later. It supports NASA’s goal of introducing more students to aerospace careers.

| Player experience | NASA evidence and the simplification |
| --- | --- |
| Brush dust from the Mars array. Compare its estimated output as cells clear, then verify the power available to the habitat. | On sol 884, InSight recovered about 30 watt-hours per sol after windblown sand helped lift dust. The game uses a brush and power blocks. [NASA JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-insight-mars-lander-gets-a-power-boost/) |
| Tune the Mars antenna until it locks. Explain why a readable signal still takes time to arrive. | NASA’s relay network routes data through orbiters. Earth–Mars radio travel time varies from about 3 to 22 minutes one way. The game simplifies antenna pointing and compresses time. [NASA Mars Relay Network](https://science.nasa.gov/mars/mars-relay-network/) |
| Seal a leak and check the pressure trend. | In 2018 the station crew located and repaired a roughly 2 mm hole in a docked Soyuz. The three-seal task and pressure readout are simplified. [NASA station update](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2018/08/30/international-space-station-status-2/) |
| Read a flare log and reconnect the ship’s power bus. | The May 14, 2024 flare reached X8.7. The electrical fault in the game is fictional. [NASA flare report](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/solar-cycle-25/2024/05/14/strong-solar-flare-erupts-from-sun-15/) |
| Bring the backup cooling loop into its operating band. | Challenger lost a main engine about 5 minutes 45 seconds into STS-51F and reached orbit using an alternate flight path. The in-game cooling repair is fictional. [NASA STS-51F](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/sts-51F/) |
| Compare Apollo 13 cabin CO₂ readings and fit a backup scrubber cartridge. | NASA’s technical brief records the CO₂ buildup and how the adapted scrubber reduced it. The in-game cartridge steps simplify the crew’s workaround. [NASA CO₂ technical brief](https://www.nasa.gov/ochmo-tb-004-carbon-dioxide-2/) |
| Bring backup heat shield segments online and check hull integrity before Earth entry. | Artemis I returned safely. NASA later investigated unexpected loss of charred heat shield material. The backup switches are fictional. [NASA Artemis I investigation](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/nasa-identifies-cause-of-artemis-i-orion-heat-shield-char-loss/) |

**A short demo:** Show a dusty panel’s low output, brush it, then show the real increase to the game’s power budget. Jump to the return flight and show what the team reads and does at the Apollo 13 station. End on the repair report and Space Careers link. NASA’s STEM Engagement program identifies career-connected learning as one way to reach students. [NASA STEM Engagement](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement/)

**How to check whether it works:** Ask students before and after playing to explain a new term and apply it to a different situation. For example, ask why a clean array still loses output in a storm, or why a stronger radio signal cannot make a reply arrive instantly. Have them identify one aerospace job they would like to learn more about. Track correct explanations and changes in interest. This can measure short-term learning and interest. Career choices and recruitment would require longer follow-up.
